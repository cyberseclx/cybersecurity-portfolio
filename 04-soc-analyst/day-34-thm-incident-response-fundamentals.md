## Event, Alert, and Incident

| Term | Practical meaning |
|---|---|
| **Event** | An observable occurrence in a system, application, account, or network. |
| **Alert** | A signal generated by a tool or person that requires validation and triage. |
| **Incident** | A confirmed or reasonably suspected security compromise, policy violation, or harmful event requiring coordinated response. |

Not every event is suspicious, and not every alert becomes an incident. An analyst must validate the evidence, establish context, estimate impact, and determine the appropriate escalation.

## Incident Severity and Prioritization

Severity should be based on evidence and business context rather than the alert name alone. Useful factors include:

- Criticality of the affected asset or service
- Sensitivity of the affected data
- Number of systems, accounts, or users involved
- Operational, financial, customer, or safety impact
- Evidence of an active attacker, persistence, or lateral movement
- Legal, regulatory, contractual, or reporting requirements

Severity definitions and escalation targets vary between organizations, so they should be documented in the incident response plan.

## Common Incident Types

- Phishing and business email compromise
- Credential theft and account compromise
- Malware and ransomware
- Unauthorized access or privilege abuse
- Data exposure or data exfiltration
- Web application attacks
- Denial-of-service attacks
- Insider misuse

## SANS Incident Response Process – PICERL

| Phase | Analyst focus |
|---|---|
| **Preparation** | Define roles, contacts, logging, tools, backups, playbooks, training, and communication procedures. |
| **Identification** | Validate the alert, collect evidence, build an initial timeline, determine scope, classify the incident, and assign severity. |
| **Containment** | Limit damage by isolating affected hosts, disabling compromised accounts, blocking malicious indicators, or segmenting traffic. |
| **Eradication** | Remove malware and persistence, correct the root cause, patch vulnerabilities, and reset compromised credentials. |
| **Recovery** | Restore clean systems and services, validate normal operation, and monitor closely for recurrence. |
| **Lessons Learned** | Review the timeline and decisions, record the root cause and control gaps, and improve procedures, detections, and playbooks. |

**Memory line:** Prepare → Identify → Contain → Eradicate → Recover → Learn.

Containment limits immediate harm. Eradication removes the cause and persistence. Recovery returns systems to normal operation. These stages should not be treated as interchangeable.

## NIST Incident Response Lifecycle

The established NIST SP 800-61 lifecycle is commonly presented as:

1. Preparation
2. Detection and Analysis
3. Containment, Eradication, and Recovery
4. Post-Incident Activity

NIST SP 800-61 Revision 3 now places incident response within the NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0. In that model, incident response centres on **Detect, Respond, and Recover**, supported by wider preparation activities across **Govern, Identify, and Protect**, followed by continuous improvement.

## SANS and NIST Mapping

| SANS phase | Closest established NIST phase |
|---|---|
| Preparation | Preparation |
| Identification | Detection and Analysis |
| Containment | Containment, Eradication, and Recovery |
| Eradication | Containment, Eradication, and Recovery |
| Recovery | Containment, Eradication, and Recovery |
| Lessons Learned | Post-Incident Activity |

The labels differ, but both approaches require preparation, detection and analysis, controlled remediation, recovery, documentation, and improvement.

## Detection and Response Tools

| Tool or data source | Incident response value |
|---|---|
| **SIEM** | Centralizes logs, correlates activity, and supports alerting, searches, and timelines. |
| **EDR** | Provides endpoint telemetry and may support host isolation, process investigation, and remediation. |
| **IDS/IPS** | Detects or blocks suspicious network activity. |
| **Firewall, proxy, and DNS logs** | Provide network destinations, connection history, filtering actions, and domain-resolution context. |
| **Email security tools** | Help investigate senders, links, attachments, delivery, and affected recipients. |
| **Threat intelligence** | Enriches indicators with reputation and campaign context; it does not replace validation. |
| **Forensic tools** | Preserve and analyse evidence from disks, memory, files, and other artefacts. |
| **Case-management system** | Records ownership, evidence, decisions, timestamps, communications, and actions. |
| **Backups** | Support recovery after integrity and cleanliness have been verified. |

## Incident Response Plan vs Playbook

### Incident Response Plan

An incident response plan defines the organization-wide structure for handling incidents, including:

- Authority, responsibilities, and escalation paths
- Incident classification and severity levels
- Internal and external communication procedures
- Evidence handling and documentation requirements
- Legal, regulatory, privacy, and third-party coordination
- Recovery priorities and business continuity dependencies

### Playbook

A playbook provides repeatable, incident-specific actions for scenarios such as phishing, ransomware, credential compromise, or data exfiltration. It should define the required evidence, triage questions, decision points, containment options, escalation conditions, and recovery checks.

The plan defines **who, when, and under what authority**. A playbook defines **how to handle a particular incident type**.

## Practical Response Checklist

1. Validate the alert and record the initial facts.
2. Establish an incident timeline and determine the likely scope.
3. Preserve relevant evidence before taking actions that could destroy it.
4. Assign severity and notify the correct stakeholders.
5. Contain the incident using actions proportional to the risk.
6. Remove the root cause, malicious artefacts, persistence, and compromised access.
7. Restore clean systems and verify normal service operation.
8. Monitor for recurrence or previously missed indicators.
9. Document the evidence, decisions, actions, owners, and timestamps.
10. Conduct a lessons-learned review and update controls and playbooks.

Where evidence may support legal or disciplinary action, its integrity and handling history should be documented carefully. Hashes and chain-of-custody records may be required depending on the case and organizational policy.

## Guided Lab Takeaways

The guided room reinforced that incident response is a coordinated process rather than a single technical action. Effective response depends on:

- Validating and scoping before declaring conclusions
- Recording evidence before making disruptive changes
- Choosing containment actions that limit damage without creating unnecessary business impact
- Separating containment, eradication, and recovery
- Maintaining a clear timeline and escalation record
- Converting lessons learned into better controls and playbooks

## SOC Relevance

For a junior SOC analyst, the main responsibilities are to:

- Validate alerts using available telemetry and context
- Collect the minimum evidence needed to explain what happened
- Identify affected users, hosts, accounts, data, and time ranges
- Follow the correct playbook and escalation path
- Avoid making destructive changes without authorization
- Document findings clearly so another responder can continue the case
- Continue monitoring after recovery for signs of recurrence

Incident response may also require coordination with IT operations, management, legal, privacy, HR, communications, vendors, and law enforcement depending on the incident.

## Mistakes to Avoid

- Treating every alert as a confirmed compromise
- Deleting or quarantining evidence before preserving what is needed
- Containing one host without checking the wider scope
- Restoring systems before removing the cause or persistence
- Resetting credentials without investigating how they were compromised
- Failing to monitor after recovery
- Keeping incomplete notes or an unreliable timeline
- Skipping the post-incident review
